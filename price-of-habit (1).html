People who've done it

Real, well-documented stories — proof that quitting doesn't close doors, it tends to open them.

SJ

Samuel L. Jackson

Alcohol & drugs · sober since 1991

After nearly two decades of heavy drinking and drug use, Jackson entered rehab in 1991. Two weeks later, still early in recovery, he took the role that gave his career its first real breakthrough. He's been open for decades about crediting his sobriety directly with the career that followed — one that made him one of the highest-grossing actors in film history.

BO

Barack Obama

Smoking · quit around 2010

Obama smoked on and off for decades and tried to quit more than once, including a public pledge during his 2008 campaign. It took years of relapses before it finally stuck — Michelle Obama confirmed in 2011 that he'd been smoke-free for close to a year. He's since spoken publicly about how hard it was, and encouraged others trying to quit not to be discouraged by setbacks.

EJ

Elton John

Alcohol & cocaine · sober since 1990

After years of addiction that he's said nearly cost him his life, Elton John entered treatment in 1990 and has been sober ever since. He's spoken openly about how getting sober let him reconnect with his music and his purpose — and he's gone on to mentor other artists, including Eminem, through their own recovery.

RD

Robert Downey Jr.

Drugs · sober since 2003

After years of arrests, relapses, and being considered too much of a risk to insure for film roles, Downey committed to sobriety in 2003 following an ultimatum from his now-wife. A friend personally covered his insurance so he could work again — he went on to become one of the highest-paid actors in the world.

AH

Anthony Hopkins

Alcohol · sober since 1975

After a night of drinking and driving he couldn't even remember, Hopkins asked for help and had his last drink on December 29, 1975. Decades later, he still marks the date every year and continues to encourage others struggling with alcohol to reach out — "it's not unique," he's said, "there are thousands of people like me."

Every recovery looks different, and these stories do not diminish how difficult quitting can be. They serve as a reminder that people who have contended with far more than a daily habit have emerged the other side and built something worthwhile.

What tends to occur when you quit

General patterns observed after cutting out smoking, vaping, or drinking — everyone's timeline differs, and this is not a guarantee or medical advice.

20 minutesHeart rate and blood pressure start to come back down.
12 hoursCarbon monoxide levels in the blood return to normal, freeing up oxygen.
A few daysSleep quality often starts to improve as the body adjusts.
2–12 weeksCirculation improves and breathing tends to get easier.
1–9 monthsCoughing and shortness of breath typically decrease as lung function recovers.
1 yearRisk of heart disease drops to roughly half that of someone who still smokes.
5–15 yearsRisk of stroke and several cancers continues falling toward that of someone who never smoked.

Why this website exists

Price of Habit was built for one reason: to help people see, in real numbers, what an everyday habit — smoking, drinking, vaping, fast food, sugary drinks, coffee, or subscriptions — is actually costing them, and to support anyone who's trying to cut back or quit. Seeing the number is often the first honest step.

Tobacco and alcohol use have both been falling worldwide for years — proof that quitting is happening, at scale, all over the world. The other habits above aren't addictions, but the same pattern holds everywhere: the cost rarely feels real until someone actually adds it up. Wherever you are, this site is meant to make the "why" feel a little more concrete.

Why is this worth tracking?

Small, repeated purchases seldom feel like "spending" in the moment — a pack, a round, a pod each feel like an isolated decision rather than part of a running total. That is precisely why the true cost is so readily underestimated. Observing it accumulate, day by day, is the purpose of this entire site: it transforms an everyday habit into a figure you may genuinely weigh against everything else that money could achieve.

Smoking

Cigarettes contain nicotine, which is highly addictive — that's a big part of why the cost keeps recurring instead of stopping on its own.

Alcohol

Drinks bought out (bars, restaurants) usually cost several times what the same drink costs at home, so the habit's true price is often higher than it feels.

Vaping

Vaping is often assumed to be the cheaper option, but frequent pod or e-liquid replacement can add up to a similar cost as smoking.

Fast food & takeaway

A single order rarely feels big, but ordering a few times a week quietly rivals a grocery budget over a year.

Sugary drinks

Sodas and sweet drinks are usually the cheapest item on a menu, which is exactly why the daily habit is easy to overlook.

Coffee

Coffee shop prices are several times the cost of brewing at home — small on its own, but a real weekly total.

Subscriptions

Monthly charges are easy to forget about because they're automatic — that's what makes unused ones so costly.
